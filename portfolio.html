The Universe Is a Bruise on the Skin of Nothingness

The universe is just a bruise: some swell
of color blooming where there should be none—
a violet ache, a gold the void can't tell
from wound or wonder. See how it was done:

some sudden blow against the skin of dark,
impact without a fist, without a why,
just pressure, and then light—a purple mark
spread thin across the nothing, vast as sky.

And tender still. The stars are still fresh pain,
bright yellow edges where the blood runs new.
Galaxies: the mottled, bruised terrain
that swells and softens into deeper blue.

We live inside the injury—brief guest
within the throb, the swell, the slow un-pressed.

The universe reimagined as accidental wound—a bruise bloomed spontaneously on nothingness. We inhabit this tender damage, temporary guests within something that swells and will fade.

This sonnet reframes existence through injury and impermanence rather than intentional creation. The bruise metaphor transforms cosmic beauty into pathology, yet embraces our transience with gentle acceptance.

Phantom Doctorate

My right hand aches tonight—the surgeon's hand
I never grew, the one that learned to feel
for tumors, stitch the bleeder, understand
the liver's weight. My neurons fire to seal
an incision in air, reach for the steel
of scalpel-blade that isn't there. The brain
still sends its signals, makes the fingers real
enough to itch beneath a phantom glove. The pain
is sharper since the hand was never slain
but stillborn—never lived enough to grieve.
I scratch at nothing, try to cup the vein
I should be clamping, reach for the reprieve
of morphine I can't dose. My mind believes
in limbs it built from paths I didn't leave.

Phantom limb syndrome for a life never lived—neurons fire to control a surgeon's hand never grown, reaching for instruments never held. The brain built pathways for an unlived medical career.

Using medical phenomenon to explore unlived potential. The mind can't distinguish between memories of what happened and vivid imagination of what might have been. We're haunted by our unrealized neural architecture.

Mid-Molt

The snake hangs half-entombed in what it was—
old scales still clinging to its throat like gauze,
while new skin underneath bleeds raw and red,
split down the spine, a seam of pain that spreads
the more it writhes. It cannot slip back in
to what it's torn; the molt has scraped the skin
too far to heal. And yet the old still grips,
a suffocating sleeve around its ribs—
so growth becomes a slow and brutal birth,
no gentle metamorphosis, but dirt-
floor thrashing in the in-between, a state
of neither-this-nor-that, too late to wait,
too soon to be reborn. The snake must tear
itself from self—there is no other prayer.

A snake caught mid-molt—half-entombed in old scales, new skin bleeding raw beneath. Trapped in violent in-between: too late to reverse, too soon to be reborn. Growth demands self-tearing.

Growth as violence through snake shedding skin. The poem rejects romanticized transformation narratives, insisting on the brutal reality: tearing, bleeding, suffocation between incompatible forms. No gentle way through change.

The Microscope Slide

To see the cell, first flood it still with death—
formaldehyde to choke each living breath,
then crystal violet stain to dye it blue
(what moves eludes the eye; corpses show through).
Now slice it with a blade: one layer thin,
translucent as a widow's veil of skin,
and press it flat beneath the coverglass—
the weight of looking crushes what will pass
beneath our magnifying gaze. We kill
to clarify. The lens demands the still,
the fixed, the murdered specimen, the stain
of chemicals where living blood once ran.
Each truth we grasp required a sacrifice:
attention is a kind of homicide.

Preparing a microscope slide requires killing the specimen—formaldehyde, crystal violet, slicing thin, pressing flat. What moves cannot be seen; only death makes structures visible. Attention is homicide.

Attention as violence using microscope preparation as metaphor. The lens demands stillness, fixation, murder. Knowledge demands we stop what we wish to understand, trap it under glass. Clarity inseparable from violation.

I. God Fears the Silence After Prayer

Does God grow smaller when we cease to speak?
When altars cool to stone and tongues go slack,
When every throat that screamed for Him grows weak
And tips its empty bowl of pleading back?

Divinity requires our repetition—
The asking shapes what otherwise would thin
Past form, past center, just the vast attrition
Of being stretched past where a self begins.

The silence after prayer's where God dissolves:
No axis, only endless dark eclipse,
Consciousness unwound past what resolves—
God swallowing His own void through our lips.

    We think that heaven answers when we pray—
    But God's the sum of every word we say.

Radical reversal: prayers don't reach God—they create God. When humanity stops praying, divinity dissolves. God exists only as the sum of human invocation and repetition.

Inverts traditional theology by suggesting God needs humanity more than we need God. Divinity as fragile, held together only by constant human attention. We don't discover God through prayer; we speak God into being.

II. The Body Mourns the Soul

The body never wanted to be empty.
It remembers when the soul still lived here—
Back when the lungs had reasons, twenty
Thousand breaths a day drawn not from fear

Of stopping, but because you asked for air.
The heart keeps time to nothing now. It beats
Because that's what hearts do. But you're not there
To need the blood. The pulse just repeats

Its old obedience, a rhythm left
On loop. The meat goes on. And I'm bereft—
Not dead, but purposeless. Deserted. All
This breathing with no one left to appall

    Or comfort. Flesh that worked for something once.
    Now just machinery. Abundance. Dunce.

The body speaks to its departed soul, expressing grief over abandonment. The flesh remembers being animated by consciousness, having purpose beyond mere biological function. Now emptied, it continues mechanically while mourning meaning lost.

Explores the dependency between body and soul from flesh's perspective. The body didn't just house the soul—it worked for the soul, drew breath because the soul asked for air. Without consciousness, biology becomes purposeless machinery.

III. The Soul's Argument with Eternity

God granted me forever. This, the gift
I didn't ask for: consciousness that will not cease,
Awareness welded permanent. No shift
From being into sleep's forgetful peace.

I envy ash. I envy ice. The snow
That briefly holds a shape then lets it go.
I envy water's amnesia, how a river
Forgets itself by evening. Even light—quick giver,

Quick-spent—ends when it reaches an eye.
But I continue. God, I will not die.
And this, you called a blessing. This, the crown:
To be and be and be and not lie down.

    I'd trade eternity to be the smoke
    That rises once from wood, then breaks, then broke.

The soul, granted immortality as supposed gift, speaks of its horror at endless existence. It envies everything that gets to end—ash, ice, water, light. Immortality experienced as inescapable prison of consciousness.

Interrogates immortality as curse rather than blessing. The soul desires the mercy of ending, the peace of cessation. Temporary things have the gift of completion; eternal consciousness is trapped in perpetual awareness without release.

IV. Satan's Return

They say you fell, Lucifer. But I think you were placed.
Someone had to hold the edge, to mark the border
Where heaven stops. Someone had to be disgraced
So grace could have a shape, an order.

Without you burning at the rim of everything,
What would righteousness mean? What would mercy be
If there was no one past its reach? You are the hinge
On which the whole theology

Swings. Damnation is the price of choice. You paid it
Not because you lost, but so the rest could win.
The math required a minus. Heaven's weight needed
A counterbalance. Sin

    Needed a name, a face, a place to go.
    Lucifer: the boundary. The necessary no.

Addresses Lucifer directly, reframing eternal damnation not as punishment but as structural necessity. Satan must remain fallen to give meaning to grace, choice, mercy. His damnation is functional—the boundary defining heaven's limits.

Reconceptualizes the Fall as placement rather than punishment. Satan becomes necessary infrastructure for the entire theological system. Without damnation, salvation has no meaning. The math required a minus; heaven's weight needed counterbalance.

What the Gods Will Never Know

The gods have tasted every fruit that grows, have carved their names
in a thousand different woods, have known a thousand lovers,
and remember none with any clarity. Variety is the amnesia of heaven.
But I—I know the forty-seventh step where granite meets my shoulder
with a coolness that subsides. I know the ten-thousandth inch of stone
the way a blind man knows his daughter's face.

They think the punishment is the weight. It is not the weight.
It is not even the climbing.

The punishment they designed was incompleteness—to come so close
to the summit that my fingertips might brush the final edge,
only to watch it go. But here is what they failed to understand:
the boulder does not fall away from me. I release it.

That moment—that high and impossible moment when my hands let go—
that is the only honest thing in all of existence.
The gods hoard and cling and call it love.
I release. I watch it tumble. I know the particular music
of its descent the way a mother knows her child's specific cry in a crowd of thousands.

And then—oh, then—the descent. The walking down.
The gods have never walked down a mountain with empty hands,
with the lightness of a man who has nothing left to lose because he has already lost it
ten thousand times before and will lose it ten thousand times again.

This is the thing they cannot take from me:
I know one true thing infinitely. They know infinite things, once.
My boulder has a name I will not speak. It has a weight I could describe
for a hundred years and never repeat myself.

Let them have their wine and their eternity.
I have this: the walk back down. The empty hands. The faithful stone below,
waiting as it always waits.
The gods have lovers.
I have a promise that cannot be broken.

Sisyphus speaks of his punishment, revealing he has found profound meaning in repetition rather than despair. He knows every step, every inch of stone with depth the variety-drunk gods can never comprehend.

Inverts the traditional Sisyphus myth by reframing eternal repetition as source of meaning. Depth of experience, not breadth, creates meaning. The gods' punishment backfires: he transforms curse into dignity through acceptance and intimate knowledge.

Phlogiston

My mother called tonight. The way she said my name—
that soft, familiar break in the first syllable—undid me.
I wanted to explain it: the neurochemical surge, the oxytocin,
the evolutionary advantage of mammalian bonding,
how love is just the brain's efficient shorthand for genetic perpetuation.

I wanted to tell her that what she feels when she says my name
is only the firing of specific neurons in the anterior cingulate cortex,
that maternal attachment is an adaptive strategy,
that when I hear her voice break with what she calls love,
I am only responding to learned emotional patterns.

I wanted to be clinical. To dissect the tenderness.
To say: *we are mammals. This is biology. There is no ghost, no soul,
no metaphysical cord still tying me to the body I once lived inside.*
We have been explained. We are chemistry.
Phlogiston was a theory of fire before we understood combustion;
love is the last phlogiston, the thing we feel but cannot justify
now that we know what we are.

But then she laughed—at something I said, some small and stupid joke—
and I heard her all the way through the phone,
across the hundreds of miles and decades,
and the explanation fell apart in my throat.

So I said, *I love you too.*
And meant it.
And didn't know why.

A phone call from the speaker's mother becomes occasion to examine love through neuroscience. The speaker intellectually accepts that emotions are electrochemical processes—yet responds with genuine feeling anyway, caught between scientific truth and lived experience.

Explores tension between reductive materialism and felt experience. Love as "the last phlogiston"—outdated theory persisting despite scientific explanation. The poem acknowledges the brain's mechanisms while recognizing that explanation doesn't eliminate the phenomenon. Knowledge doesn't dissolve meaning.
