Summary

The soul, granted immortality as a supposed gift from God, speaks of its horror at endless existence. Rather than celebrating eternal life, the soul envies everything that gets to end—ash, ice, water, even light itself. Trapped in perpetual awareness, the soul experiences immortality not as paradise but as an inescapable prison of consciousness.

Themes

This poem inverts the traditional view of immortality as blessing, portraying eternal existence as unbearable torture. The soul envies mortality—the capacity for complete dissolution and rest that death offers. Deathlessness becomes "the noose," a tightening knot that grows more constrictive over infinite time. The poem explores the paradox of eternal consciousness: the soul is "the dreamer always caught here," aware it's dreaming but unable to wake or truly sleep. This creates a hall-of-mirrors effect where the conscious self watches itself watching itself ad infinitum, with no escape. The poem challenges the notion that eternal life is desirable, suggesting that mortality's finite boundary gives meaning and mercy to existence. Heaven becomes hell when there's no release, no ending, no possibility of true transformation or rest. The soul would "murder Heaven" for the gift of mortal grief—the capacity to truly lose, truly end, and thus truly be free.

III. The Soul's Argument with Eternity

by Krishna Patel

Not death but deathlessness becomes the noose:
To be the knot that tightens over time,
To have no grave as shelter or as truce,
No dissolution waiting past this climb.

I envy ash—how thoroughly it ends,
The way ice shatters into water, water
Evaporates. Even light descends
At last. But I'm the dreamer always caught here—

Locked inside the dream who knows it's dreaming
But cannot force the dream to break or fade,
The mirror watching mirrors, never sleeping,
Never waking, just the watching, made

    Eternal. God promised forever as His gift—
    I'd murder Heaven for grief's mortal rift.

Krishna Patel
