Summary

A phone call from the speaker's mother becomes an occasion to examine love and selfhood through the lens of neuroscience and eliminative materialism. The speaker intellectually accepts that emotions, identity, and consciousness are merely electrochemical processes that will eventually be explained away—yet finds themselves responding with genuine feeling anyway, caught between scientific truth and lived experience.

Themes

The poem grapples with eliminative materialism—the philosophical position that mental states like belief, desire, and selfhood will be "eliminated" rather than reduced once neuroscience fully explains consciousness. Drawing on philosophers Paul and Patricia Churchland, the speaker acknowledges that "I," "love," and "missing" are likely just provisional labels for neural patterns, destined to become obsolete like phlogiston (the substance scientists once believed caused combustion). Yet the poem's power lies in its refusal to let this intellectual position win completely. Even while cataloging the neurochemical mechanics of emotion—oxytocin, amygdala activation, limbic response—the speaker cannot escape the subjective experience of those emotions. The ending suggests that perhaps being "beautifully mistaken" about love's reality doesn't diminish its significance. The difference between real and convincing illusion might itself be phlogiston: a distinction that will dissolve, but which continues to burn with meaning in the present moment. This explores the tension between third-person scientific description and first-person lived experience, asking whether explaining away consciousness actually eliminates it or just reframes our understanding of what it means to be human.

Phlogiston

by Krishna Patel

My mother calls to say she misses me. The neurons fire. Oxytocin floods
the ventral tegmental area. The prefrontal cortex lights up on the fMRI
like a suburb at dusk. There is no missing. There is electrical cascade,
sodium-potassium pump, the evolutionary artifact of pair-bonding
translated into what I have been calling love but is only chemistry
older than language, older than what I am calling I.

The Churchlands say belief is phlogiston—the substance we invented
to explain why things burn, before we understood combustion.
Folk psychology, they call it. The Theater of Mind. A Bronze Age attempt
to narrate the deterministic dance of meat and synapse. When I say I want
there is no I, no want. There is only cortical activation that will be explained away
like humors, like élan vital, like the soul.

My mother's voice breaks when she says my name. Or: the acoustical vibrations
trigger the amygdala's stored associations: warmth, safety, childhood,
the word for someone who no longer exists as a coherent self but only
as a pattern we mistake for permanence.

I should tell her this. I should say: Mother, there is no missing because
there is no me to miss or you to do the missing. There are neurotransmitters
firing in predictable sequences that we have mistakenly anthropomorphized
into grief. But something—some cluster of cells activated by seventy thousand years
of evolutionary pressure to maintain the fiction of connection—

says, I miss you too.

And means it. Or: the neural correlates of sincerity activate in the pattern
we have been calling meaning but which is only another name for what the brain does
when it believes in its own story hard enough to act.

The Churchlands are probably right. In fifty years we will look back at "I"
the way we look back at phlogiston: a quaint attempt to fill the explanatory gap
with narrative. Consciousness will be eliminated, not reduced. We will have better words.
More accurate maps of the electrical storm we are calling self.

But here, now, in what I am calling the present—this moment that is only
neural binding, synchronized gamma oscillations giving the illusion of nowness—
I feel my throat tighten. The limbic system activates. Salt water accumulates
in the lacrimal gland because

my mother called to say she misses me and I don't have better words than love.

The truth is probably that there is no truth, only brain states we haven't finished
explaining. But the other truth—the one that doesn't exist except as computation—is this:

when she says my name I am someone. When she says I love you there is an I
and a you and something between us that is either real or so beautifully mistaken
for real that the difference is phlogiston:

gone but still burning. Still burning.

Krishna Patel
