Sonnet Type

Petrarchan

Summary

The speaker describes a snake caught mid-molt, half-entombed in its old scales while new skin bleeds raw underneath. The old scales cling like gauze around its throat, a suffocating sleeve around its ribs. The snake cannot slip back into what it's torn from, yet cannot complete the transformation either. It writhes in dirt-floor agony, trapped in the violent in-between: too late to wait, too soon to be reborn. The only path forward is brutal self-tearing.

Themes

This Petrarchan sonnet explores growth as violence through the metaphor of a snake shedding skin. The octave establishes the physical horror: the snake is "half-entombed," old scales clinging like gauze while new skin bleeds raw and red beneath, "split down the spine, a seam of pain that spreads." The molt is irreversible—"It cannot slip back in / to what it's torn"—yet incomplete, leaving the creature trapped in a suffocating in-between state. The sestet shifts from physical description to existential condition: growth becomes "a slow and brutal birth, / no gentle metamorphosis," reducing the snake to "dirt- / floor thrashing" in the state of "neither-this-nor-that." The poem rejects romanticized narratives of transformation as smooth evolution or beautiful becoming. Instead, it insists on the violence inherent in change: the tearing, bleeding, suffocation of being caught between incompatible forms. The final line delivers the poem's devastating wisdom: "The snake must tear / itself from self—there is no other prayer." Growth demands self-violence, self-annihilation. There is no gentle way through transformation, no external force to complete what you've started. The only prayer is the brutal act of tearing yourself free from what you were. This speaks to broader themes of identity, becoming, and the necessary destruction that accompanies all meaningful change.

Mid-Molt

by Krishna Patel

The snake hangs half-entombed in what it was—
old scales still clinging to its throat like gauze,
while new skin underneath bleeds raw and red,
split down the spine, a seam of pain that spreads
the more it writhes. It cannot slip back in
to what it's torn; the molt has scraped the skin
too far to heal. And yet the old still grips,
a suffocating sleeve around its ribs—
so growth becomes a slow and brutal birth,
no gentle metamorphosis, but dirt-
floor thrashing in the in-between, a state
of neither-this-nor-that, too late to wait,
too soon to be reborn. The snake must tear
itself from self—there is no other prayer.

Krishna Patel
