Spenserian

Summary

The speaker experiences phantom limb syndrome—but for a life never lived. Their neurons fire to control a surgeon's hand they never grew, reaching for instruments that were never held, performing procedures never learned. The brain built neural pathways for an unlived medical career, creating a phantom doctorate that aches more sharply because it was stillborn rather than amputated.

Themes

This Spenserian sonnet uses the medical phenomenon of phantom limbs to explore unlived potential and roads not taken. The central metaphor is devastating: just as amputees feel sensations in missing limbs because the brain's neural map remains intact, the speaker experiences phantom sensations for an entire life path their brain anticipated but never manifested. Their neurons "fire to seal / an incision in air," reaching for instruments that don't exist, itching beneath gloves never worn. The poem suggests that our brains build infrastructure for futures we imagine—and those abandoned possibilities leave neurological scars. The pain is "sharper since the hand was never slain / but stillborn," meaning grief for unlived lives cuts deeper than mourning what was lost, because there's nothing concrete to grieve. The speaker can't dose morphine that doesn't exist, can't clamp veins they never learned to find. The final couplet crystallizes the tragedy: "My mind believes / in limbs it built from paths I didn't leave"—suggesting we become haunted by our own unrealized neural architecture, by the person our brain prepared us to become but we never became. This explores themes of regret, potential, the neurology of imagination, and how our minds can't distinguish between memories of what happened and vivid imagination of what might have been.

Phantom Doctorate

by Krishna Patel

My right hand aches tonight—the surgeon's hand
I never grew, the one that learned to feel
for tumors, stitch the bleeder, understand
the liver's weight. My neurons fire to seal
an incision in air, reach for the steel
of scalpel-blade that isn't there. The brain
still sends its signals, makes the fingers real
enough to itch beneath a phantom glove. The pain
is sharper since the hand was never slain
but stillborn—never lived enough to grieve.
I scratch at nothing, try to cup the vein
I should be clamping, reach for the reprieve
of morphine I can't dose. My mind believes
in limbs it built from paths I didn't leave.

Krishna Patel
