Shakespearean

Summary

The speaker describes preparing a biological specimen for microscopic examination, recognizing that the act of observation requires killing the subject. To see clearly—to illuminate cellular structures and make the invisible visible—they must first fix, stain, and slice the living tissue paper-thin. The poem equates scientific attention with a kind of intimate violence.

Themes

This Shakespearean sonnet explores attention as a form of violence, using the concrete process of preparing microscope slides as metaphor for how observation transforms and destroys its subject. The speaker must "kill it first to see it clear"—must arrest life's movement to study its structure. Each step of specimen preparation (fixation, sectioning, staining) is both scientific necessity and small murder. The act of making something visible requires rendering it static, dead, consumable by the eye. This raises questions about the cost of knowledge and whether true understanding requires destruction of the living, breathing thing. The poem suggests that clarity and truth come at the price of vitality—we can only see the organism's inner workings after we've stopped its processes. The final couplet acknowledges the contradiction: "I learn by what I've killed. I see / by making sure it cannot look at me," suggesting that knowledge requires power asymmetry, that the observer must dominate and silence the observed. This speaks to broader themes about how scrutiny objectifies, how analysis dissects, and how the scientific gaze requires eliminating the subject's agency and aliveness. Attention, taken to its extreme, becomes autopsy.

The Microscope Slide

by Krishna Patel

To see the cell, I have to kill it first—
Fix it in formalin, slice it paper-thin,
Stain the nucleus until it bursts
Into color the living never wore within.

The mitochondria glow like fairy lights
Only after I've stopped their motion cold.
The membrane walls—transparent in life—ignite
With crystal violet, brilliant, dead, and bold.

This is the cost of clarity: the blade,
The fixing agent, the dye that makes it sing.
To understand the architecture, invade
The house and murder every living thing.

    I learn by what I've killed. I see
    By making sure it cannot look at me.

Krishna Patel
