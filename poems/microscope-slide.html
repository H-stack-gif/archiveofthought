Summary

The speaker methodically describes the process of preparing a microscope slide: flooding the cell with death (formaldehyde), staining it with crystal violet to make the invisible visible, slicing it translucent-thin, and pressing it flat beneath coverglass. Each step requires killing what was once living, because "what moves eludes the eye; corpses show through." The poem concludes that scientific observation demands stillness, fixation, murder—that attention is a kind of homicide.

Themes

This Shakespearean sonnet explores attention as a form of violence, using the concrete process of preparing microscope slides as metaphor for how observation transforms and destroys its subject. The lens demands "the still, / the fixed, the murdered specimen"—life's movement must be arrested to study its structure. Each step of preparation (flooding with formaldehyde, staining with crystal violet, slicing thin, pressing flat) is both scientific necessity and methodical killing. What moves cannot be seen; only death makes cellular structures visible. The poem interrogates the cost of knowledge: we can only see by making the living dead, only understand by destroying what we study. The final couplet delivers the moral reckoning: "Each truth we grasp required a sacrifice: / attention is a kind of homicide." This extends beyond microscopy to broader questions about scrutiny, analysis, and the scientific gaze. All deep observation requires objectification—turning the living subject into fixed object, the dynamic into static, the whole into dissected parts. Knowledge demands we stop the thing we wish to understand, trap it under glass, reduce it to stained chemicals. The poem suggests that clarity and insight are inseparable from violation, that to truly see something we must first kill it.

The Microscope Slide

by Krishna Patel

To see the cell, first flood it still with death—
formaldehyde to choke each living breath,
then crystal violet stain to dye it blue
(what moves eludes the eye; corpses show through).
Now slice it with a blade: one layer thin,
translucent as a widow's veil of skin,
and press it flat beneath the coverglass—
the weight of looking crushes what will pass
beneath our magnifying gaze. We kill
to clarify. The lens demands the still,
the fixed, the murdered specimen, the stain
of chemicals where living blood once ran.
Each truth we grasp required a sacrifice:
attention is a kind of homicide.

Krishna Patel
